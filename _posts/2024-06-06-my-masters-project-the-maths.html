Part I: Category Theory

My project started as an attempt to understand category theory and its uses in mathematics. At the start of almost any undergraduate mathematics education, students like me will be seeing some kind of “set theory” and how to write proofs with it. Set theory is a basic tool for essentially all mathematicians to form and tackle problems: how many elements are there in a set? If I know two sets A and B, how can one construct functions from A to B? Are they injective and/or surjective (or both, i.e. bijective)? Working with those should be very familiar.

However, as one studies more abstract algebra (a branch of mathematics, it is not the “Algebra” you might be expecting in high school!), one will meet way more structure and their “structure-preserving transformations”. For example:

  • Groups and Group isomorphisms/homomorphisms,
  • Vector spaces and Isomorphic Vector spaces / linear maps,
  • Rings with Ring isomorphism/homomorphism,

(My non-math friends probably will lose it here so early into the passage, I am sorry…) It is quite a lot of definitions to go through, but you can bring one level up in abstraction in category theory!

Category Theory enriches (hopefully this is the right word) set theory to allow us to discuss more possibilities in mathematics. I probably cannot say it better than Oliver Lugg’s video. But in my paper, I also mentioned two working principles, which make category theory very versatile.

  1. The idea of duality. Morphisms (or maps) in a category have a source (say A) and a target (say B), but consider maps from A to B, since we just “call them” A and B, the theory makes no difference if we consider maps from B to A. That is, we “say” the “target” is where the maps start and the “source” is where the maps end. We just “say” it is so, so we would not expect the idea would be vastly different. For math people again, this actually tackles a lot of annoying bookkeeping in topics such as dual spaces, left-right module differences, etc.
  2. The idea of universal properties. Sometimes, one might be presented with a mathematical object and don’t know what can be done with it. A good idea is to look at maps coming out / going into this object (and by duality concerns, any of the two would be fine). It turns out that this practice is enough for all practical purposes (the jargon is you can always get the object “up to isomorphism”). An example for the math people again: given two (finite-dimensional) vector spaces V and W, how could we understand the direct sum V+W? Well, one could say if I give you any extra vector space T, and two linear maps f: V to T, and g: W to T, then I can have a “natural” map h: V + W to T (what do you think it is?), this map completely tells me how V+W “behaves”. In fact, the key lemma in Category theory, the Yoneda Lemma, heavily generalizes this observation.

These two concepts made up my second chapter. The third chapter was quite technical, and as I am writing this blog post I feel like it is too inaccessible for the laymen and boring for the mathematicians, so I will not mention it too much. But it involves looking at adjoint functors, which is a way to transport objects between two structurally different categories. The whole first part leads to a nice theorem of “adjoint preserves limits”, but I have no time or space to explain what these words mean.

Part II: Category Theory in Algebras

Anyway, the second part puts category theory into applications in abstract algebra. In particular, I am interested in thinking about modules over non-commutative algebras. Again, the terms “modules”, “non-commutative”, and even “algebra” this time are all technical terms. I don’t know a good way to present it without getting too deep into rewriting the paper, so I would just share my common response when someone asks me what this “algebra” thing is about. For students currently doing their Masters in pure mathematics, you might want to hear that my work also includes some homological algebra.

When I told my friends I was writing a paper, some of them would ask me what is it about. I usually answer “abstract algebra”, and upon further questions, the only words that I manage to spurt out are probably “It is very abstract, I think about how things add and multiply”.

And that’s kinda about it. For the more interested readers, I worked with a special type of algebras called “quiver algebras”, where all the information can be retrieved by drawing a finite directed graph, possibly with multiple edges between two vertices and loops. I was also interested in modules over those algebras. As representation theorists are concerned, the first question is probably to ask for a decomposition of the regular module into indecomposable modules, and there is a nice one consisting of only projective modules.

Back to the normal English language, one thing that was quite unexpected in this project was that I worked a lot with pictures. This is to give a good visualization of different objects. This is also a common theme in both Part I and II of the paper.

In the final chapter, I produced a new calculation, verifying an algebra satisfies a particular condition (injective generation). Combined with my supervisor’s work, this in turn gives an example where a famous conjecture (the “Finitistic Dimension Conjecture”) holds for that algebra.
