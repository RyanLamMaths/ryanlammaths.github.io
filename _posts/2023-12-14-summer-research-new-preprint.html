Hi everyone! I am announcing a new preprint, “A coarse geometric approach to graph layout problems”, collaborated with Wanying Huang, David Hume, and Samuel J. Kelly from the Summer Research Project at the University of Bristol! I also want to discuss my experience and feelings in mathematics research.

About the Preprint

First, a little bit about the content. This is a research project supervised by Dr David Hume on graph invariants: that is, some integers that we can assign to different graphs. We always work with undirected, connected, but possibly infinite graphs.

A graph is a collection of vertex and edges between them, how can we associate it with a number?

What we are interested in is the growth of a collection of invariants, and it turns out most of them are the “same” when we look at a map of graphs with controlled “expanding” properties (what we call “kappa-regular maps” in our paper.)

I focussed on the discussion of cutwidth, bandwidth, and treewidth, and I have uploaded a separate exposition article titled “Graph Invariants and Regular Maps” here. The exposition is more tailored to undergraduate/talented high school students, by covering only two (Proposition 3.2 and 3.4) in specific cases.

My Experience

A few weeks after the project had ended, I was invited to an internship event and a project presentation, both at the Fry Building at the University of Bristol. As a panelist, I talked more about my personal experience in applying and working on this project at the internship event. I talked more about the subject content and my discoveries in the project presentation. I want to focus on the former more in this post.

I did an 8-week project throughout the summer holiday (from 1 August), and I got an office shared with Sam, Wanying, and another undergraduate student from another project. On the surface, working in research is not the most exciting thing (at least when I type this out I don’t find this particularly exciting): you try to type your own result, working 9-5 Monday to Friday, and you meet with your supervisor weekly to discuss progress and investigate more notes in draft to make sure your work is alright… probably what someone would expect when you are “working in a lab”… I suppose?

However, this doesn’t mean it is not a meaningful experience, I still have a lot of takeaways.

Main Takeaway: Research is Hard.

Although I definitely enjoy committing to reading papers and writing up mathematics, I realized the project put a real test on my mental resilience. At times I have to bang my head on an idea, only to realize it had been proven after an extensive paper search. Sometimes, an idea will not click for me for days, or weeks, even if you spend 8 hours every day looking meticulously at every word in a proof.

That being said, there are some tips and tricks I found useful during these times.

  • Proving very basic properties. When I met a new definition in a paper, I often had a “mental picture” in mind and moved on to read the rest of the paper. But it is good at this time to stop and think about how the new definition fits in with different very basic objects. In my case, consider a graph invariant, what is the invariant on a cycle? A complete graph? A bipartite graph? Working them out could give a hint of how the proof of a general statement may go.
  • Write things down (neater than you thought it might be). It is always a good idea to keep track of your progress. Sometimes when things click, you become so excited (and maybe overly obsessed) with the idea and call it a day because you understand something new. While understanding new things is always a call for celebration, do remember having a record of what you are thinking is always useful (and ultimately, it is what you want to produce at the end of your research). Moreover, there are times (more than I want to admit) where I embarrassed myself by thinking I created a new proof, just to discover that the whole idea breaks down if I write it down formally on LaTex.
  • Talk to others. I feel like this is always highly underrated in research. Making sure I have something to report and ask in my weekly supervisor meeting helps me a lot. But more important than that, talk to your colleagues and friends! When I was presenting a proof to others, I found key insights that I was missing when I was writing alone. Huge shout-outs to Samuel for finding one of the key ideas that I was missing when I was proving a key lemma!
  • Keep yourself active and happy! Doing research is no easy job, and to be fair I still have imposter syndrome lingering in any projects that I did. When I was working on the project, I also always felt like I was embarrassing myself by asking stupid questions and making ridiculous statements every time I met my supervisor. But as time passed, I tried my best to accept this is what research entails, and I have started to enjoy it. In the meantime, I also made sure that I don’t work on Saturdays and Sundays, so I really have time to rest and recharge myself during the weekends. I felt like I really had time to do some stretches, relax, and prepare for next week’s work (definitely not a foreshadowing…)
